## The sticky-release problem - what three research passes agreed on (2026-09-20)

Derek: "don't just bandaid this up - really think through it." Three agents (iOS scroll physics from WebKit's source; HCI literature + practitioner prior art; a first-principles critique of this component). Where they agree:

- **On a phone there is no "hand gain" to retrain** - touch is 1:1. A pin makes the finger slide over content that doesn't move, so people switch from dragging to FLICKING (nobody drags ten strokes through five screens); iOS boosts repeated same-direction flicks; and the fling is invisible while pinned, so it can't be caught by eye. It crosses the let-go with about half a second of momentum left - 1.5 to 3 screens.
- **The root error: "let it sink in" / "stick longer" are asks about TIME, built as scroll DISTANCE.** Distance can't hold a reader; it taxes them in flings, spent on the next section. This finale has ~3.4 screens where nothing is displaced (only opacity and color change), right after the last layer's 110vh dwell.
- **A place-based gain change only works on a hand moving slowly under visual control** (Baudisch's Snap-and-go, CHI 2005; semantic pointing, CHI 2004) - never on a fling already thrown. Matches the lab: ramps fixed the visual step, not the overshoot.
- **The web has no friction or deceleration control, shipped or proposed.** `scroll-snap-stop` is the purpose-built momentum drain, but on iOS ANY snap point on the root scroller switches every fling on the page to fast deceleration (WebKit bug 243582, open; the rule is visible in RemoteScrollingCoordinatorProxyIOS.mm) - that was the "not free scrolling" he felt.
- **A programmatic scroll DOES end an iOS fling** (WKWebViewIOS.mm stops the scroll view before setting the offset; his phone agreed - the catch was "closest"). It lands a few frames late, and writing the scroll every frame is the known stutter pattern - so: one write, and let the browser animate the rest (the soft catch, lab test F). Academic precedent for slowing the momentum phase itself at points of interest: content-aware kinetic scrolling (Kim et al., UIST 2014).
- **Nobody has written about the release moment of a pin** - not NN/g's scrolljacking study, not the GSAP forums, not the literature. The gap is real.

**The plan, in layers:** (1) remove the cause in the component - a pin of about one screen, scrub only what really moves (the widening), play the frame / line / words / color wave on TIME once it pins, and end the pin BY ARRIVAL (something moving at 1:1 reaches its seat and they leave together, so nothing the eye follows ever changes speed); the news-graphics pattern (The Pudding) is the same idea - the graphic holds while text scrolls over it at true speed, so there is always an honest moving reference. (2) the reusable piece - the soft catch at the let-go, for pins that can't be designed away. (3) landing zones - what follows a pin is deep enough to land in, and coming back up from below meets the rested figure, not the sequence in reverse. Worth borrowing from Snap-and-go: a visible cue that the end is coming. Rejected: ramps before the let-go as a fix (keep at most for visual continuity), snap on the page, taking over touch scrolling.

**The run-out (Derek, after the research: "there's no way to say 'this is almost done - get ready'... so maybe the way is to have an animated 'thing' after areas like that that kinda let you run it out a bit").** Lab test G: nothing is slowed and scrolling is untouched - after the let-go come two and a half screens with nothing to read, moving at true speed, with HORIZONTAL marks (the eye needs them to see speed again after a pin) spaced further and further apart (so the page looks like it is slowing). Why a cue can't replace it: a fling is already spent motor output, and during a pin nobody is looking at a progress bar. For this component the run-out should be made of its own material (the stripes / columns running on), not the lab's ruler marks.

## How the prototype works

- One `<practice-layers>` custom element. Each layer is a `<section>` nested inside the previous one; the parent's background showing at the left IS the stripe, so earlier layers persist.
- Story text is `position: sticky`.
- Container queries (not media queries), so it sizes to wherever it is dropped.
- (The `data-view` attribute is gone: the layout follows the container's width.)
- The component needs NO JavaScript. The script on the test page only stands in for a template (the note said "a recursive Vue component"; on this plain-PHP site that would be a recursive PHP partial fed one list).
- Layer colors are token slots (`--layer-1..n`). The `:root` tokens and the two voice classes at the top of `practice-layers.css` are stand-ins - on this site they come from the theme system and typography, and the component should consume the site's semantic slots.
- Verified in headless Chrome at phone width: all four states render, and sticky holds at 1rem after scrolling 250px into a layer (works with `container-type` on the host). **Not verified:** a real phone, and how the scroll feels.

## Gotchas

- **The test page is static HTML, so its stylesheet link carries a hand-bumped `?v=` stamp.** Forget to bump it after a CSS change and phones keep the old stylesheet against the new markup - which looks like the component is broken (unstyled names, nothing sticking). This happened on 2026-09-20.

- Sticky dies silently under any ancestor with `overflow: hidden/auto` (commented in the CSS).
- A container query cannot style its own container, so the roomier-stripes rule is set on the first `.layer`.
- Decided against GSAP/ScrollTrigger: nothing animates, the page just scrolls. If a flourish is wanted later: native CSS scroll-driven animations behind `@supports` (no Firefox support yet), decoration only.
